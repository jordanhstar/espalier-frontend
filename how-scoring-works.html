How scoring works

The Espalier Score, explained plainly

Among other filters and identifiers, every idea gets one number that sums up where it stands, produced by custom-built AI models trained specifically for this — not a generic off-the-shelf assessment — which is also able to be manually reviewed. Here's exactly what goes into it — and just as important, what doesn't.

01

Readiness

How far along the idea actually is, based on the steps the creator marked complete for its type and cross-checked by our AI models for accuracy. A working core feature, a tested prototype, a first paying customer, whatever milestones matter for that kind of idea, we include, verified against a database of typical steps for the most successful similar ideas of its type.

02

Profit potential

The size of the opportunity the creator described — a side-income project, a solid income stream, or a genuine big swing. Of course, this is not a guarantee, and it heavily depends on who the idea gets passed on to and how much time, effort, and resources they are able to put into it.

03

Cost to finish

A rough estimate of what it would actually take — in money, time, or both — to carry the idea the rest of the way. Lower isn't automatically better; it's just one more thing to weigh.

04

File-check

Espalier's proprietary AI models look at the actual files, links, and materials a creator uploads and compare that to what they claimed. If the files show more progress than claimed, the score goes up. If they show less, it goes down — the check runs both directions, so being modest doesn't cost you and overselling doesn't pay off.

05

Category risk

Some categories of idea carry more inherent execution risk than others. This is a small adjustment, not a judgment on whether the idea itself is good.

06

Creator track record

Once a creator has a few completed deals behind them, their track record starts to count in the creator's favor. It never counts against a first-time creator — everyone starts even.

What the score deliberately ignores: how original the idea is, and whether it has a social-impact angle. Both of those are shown separately on every listing — they matter for very different reasons than "how likely is this to work," so they're kept out of the number itself rather than quietly nudging it one way or another.

Why show the math at all?

Because a black-box number is just a vibe with more decimal places. If you're deciding whether to spend real time and money finishing someone else's idea, you deserve to know exactly what "74" is actually telling you — and exactly what it isn't.
